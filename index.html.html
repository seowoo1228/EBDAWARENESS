Supporting Young People With Emotional and Behavioral Disorders

A guide for community members who work with and spend time caring for children and teens.

Emotional and behavioral disorders (EBD) affect how young people manage their emotions, relationships, and behavior. These conditions are common, but most students who have them are never formally identified at school. Much of the day-to-day support these young people receive comes from adults outside of special education and mental health care.

This guide summarizes current research on EBD and describes practical strategies that community members can use. It is intended for general education and awareness. It does not replace evaluation or treatment by a qualified professional.

About this guide

The guide is organized into four sections.

  1. About EBD. Definitions, common signs, prevalence, and implications for community members.
  2. Strategies. Three evidence-based approaches: Mental Health First Aid, behavior-specific praise, and offering choices with advance reminders.
  3. Collaboration. How community members can support families and work alongside schools, counselors, and agencies.
  4. Transition to adulthood. Changes young people face after high school and factors linked to positive adult outcomes.

Section 1

About EBD

What is EBD?

Emotional and behavioral disorders is a broad term that describes long-lasting patterns of emotion or behavior that interfere with an individual’s learning, relationships, or functioning. EBD is not a diagnosis, and an individual with EBD may have a diagnosis such as anxiety, depression, and more.

Federal special education law uses the term emotional disturbance. Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), a student may qualify if they show one or more of the following characteristics over a long period of time and to a marked degree, and the condition adversely affects their educational performance (Child With a Disability, 2017):

  • An inability to learn that cannot be explained by intellectual, sensory, or health factors
  • An inability to build or maintain satisfactory relationships with peers and teachers
  • Inappropriate types of behavior or feelings under normal circumstances
  • A general, pervasive mood of unhappiness or depression
  • A tendency to develop physical symptoms or fears associated with personal or school problems

Signs and patterns

Behaviors associated with EBD are often grouped into two categories. Many young people show some of each.

Externalizing

Behaviors directed toward others or the environment.

  • Arguing, defiance, or refusing directions
  • Verbal or physical aggression
  • Rule-breaking or impulsive behavior
  • Frequent conflict with peers

Internalizing

Behaviors directed inward. These are often overlooked because they rarely disrupt others.

  • Withdrawal or social isolation
  • Persistent sadness, worry, or fear
  • Frequent headaches or stomachaches
  • Loss of interest in usual activities

How common is EBD?

  • About 12 in 100 students have emotional or behavioral concerns that warrant support at any given time (Forness et al., 2012).
  • Fewer than 1 in 100 students receives special education services under the category of emotional disturbance.
  • The remaining students who need support rely on general education teachers, families, and community members.

Long-term outcomes

Studies that follow students into adulthood have found that youth with emotional disturbances are less likely than their peers to finish school, hold steady jobs, or live independently. They also have high rates of involvement with the criminal justice system. These studies have documented some improvement over time, particularly in enrollment in education after high school (Wagner & Newman, 2012).

Implications for community members

Adult responses affect behavior

A calm and consistent response can help a young person regain control. A response that is angry or shaming can escalate the situation.

Labels can limit opportunities

A young person who is seen as a “problem kid” may be excluded from teams, programs, and activities. Treating behavior as a form of communication helps adults look for the underlying need.

Community adults may notice changes first

Coaches, relatives, and mentors often see a young person regularly over long periods. Their observations can help a family decide when to seek an evaluation.

Structured activities provide connection

Teams, clubs, and youth programs offer routine and relationships with caring adults, which many young people with EBD may not have elsewhere.

Section 2

Evidence-based strategies

Strategy 1: Mental Health First Aid

What it is

Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) is a training program that teaches community members how to recognize signs of a mental health problem, provide initial support, and connect a person with professional help. The program applies the model of physical first aid to mental health. A youth version is designed for adults who interact with adolescents.

What the research shows

A meta-analysis of 18 controlled trials with nearly 6,000 participants found that MHFA training improved participants’ knowledge, their ability to recognize mental health problems, and their confidence in helping others. Training also reduced stigma, and participants provided more help to people in need in the months after the course (Morgan et al., 2018).

How to use it

MHFA teaches a five-step action plan known as ALGEE.

  1. Approach, assess for risk, and assist. Choose a calm, private moment to check in. If there is any sign of risk to the young person or others, get help immediately.
  2. Listen nonjudgmentally. Let the young person talk without interrupting, lecturing, or minimizing their concerns.
  3. Give support and information. Let them know their feelings are valid and that help is available.
  4. Encourage appropriate professional help. For a minor, this usually means involving a parent, caregiver, or school counselor.
  5. Encourage other supports. Point them toward trusted friends, family members, and routines that help them cope.

Example: A coach may notice that a player has become withdrawn, or a teacher may notice unusual behavior from a student. The adult can check in privately, listen without judgment, and involve a parent, caregiver, or counselor if needed.

Strategy 2: Behavior-specific praise

What it is

Behavior-specific praise names the exact behavior an adult wants to see again. Instead of saying “good job,” the adult describes what the young person did well.

What the research shows

Observational studies have found that students with EBD receive very little praise, in some cases only one or two statements per hour (Wehby et al., 1995, as cited in Royer et al., 2019). A systematic review found that behavior-specific praise from teachers increased time on task and reduced inappropriate behavior, and classified it as a potentially evidence-based practice (Royer et al., 2019). A review of 50 years of research identified 57 studies of behavior-specific praise, many involving students with challenging behavior, including students with EBD (Ennis et al., 2020). Although most of this research took place in schools, the strategy can be used in sports, at home, and in the workplace.

How to use it

Effective praise names the specific behavior, is given soon after the behavior occurs, and is sincere. Select each example to see a general statement rewritten as behavior-specific praise.

“Good job today.”

“You stayed calm when the referee made that call and kept playing.”

“Thanks for being good.”

“Thank you for waiting your turn in line and keeping your hands to yourself.”

“Nice work.”

“You asked for help when the register jammed. That was the right thing to do.”

Adults should aim to give more positive feedback than correction.

Strategy 3: Offering choices and advance reminders

What it is

Many young people with EBD have little sense of control over their lives, and direct demands can lead to power struggles. Offering a choice between two acceptable options gives the young person some control while still completing the task. A reminder given before a difficult situation, sometimes called precorrection, helps the young person prepare.

What the research shows

Lane et al. (2018) describe instructional choice and precorrection as low-intensity strategies that are effective, require little time or cost, and fit into everyday routines. These strategies were developed for schools but apply to many other settings.

How to use it

Offer only choices you are willing to accept.

Instead ofTry
“Go do your warm-up.”“Do you want to start with laps or passing drills?”
“Clean your room now.”“Do you want to clean up before dinner or after?”
“Put your phone away.”“You can put your phone in your bag or on the table.”

For advance reminders, state what is about to happen and what is expected before a known difficult moment. For example: “In five minutes we are leaving the park. We will walk to the car together.” Predictable routines and advance notice of changes also reduce conflict.

Responding when a situation escalates

Recommended

  • Speak calmly and slowly
  • Give the young person physical space
  • Use few words and give one clear request
  • Discuss the situation after everyone is calm

Avoid

  • Raising your voice or arguing
  • Correcting the young person in front of others
  • Making threats you will not follow through on
  • Demanding an apology during an outburst

Section 3

Collaboration with families, counselors, and community agencies

Why coordination matters

Young people with serious emotional and behavioral needs often receive services from more than one system, such as schools, mental health providers, health care, child welfare, or juvenile justice. Each system has its own requirements and goals, and families are usually left alone to attempt to coordinate between them.

The wraparound process

Wraparound is a team-based planning process designed for young people with serious emotional and behavioral disorders and their families. The team develops a single, individualized plan. In addition to professionals, the team includes natural supports, such as relatives, friends, coaches, mentors, and faith leaders.

A meta-analysis of seven controlled studies found that youth who received wraparound had better outcomes than comparison groups, with an overall effect size of 0.33. The largest effect was on living situation, meaning youth were more likely to remain in stable, less restrictive homes. Smaller positive effects were found for mental health, overall functioning, school, and juvenile justice outcomes. However, due to the small study size, more research may be needed (Suter & Bruns, 2009).

The role of natural supports

Most community members will not attend formal team meetings. However, the principles behind wraparound apply in everyday settings. These include family voice and choice, building on strengths, respect for the family’s culture, and a commitment to continue supporting the young person through setbacks.

Working with families

Parents of children with EBD often feel judged by others, so community members can help by following the family’s lead. Ask what strategies work at home and use the same approach when possible. Share positive observations as well as concerns. Keep information the family shares confidential.

Working with schools and counselors

Privacy laws limit what schools and mental health providers can share, so information should generally pass through the family. If you notice something important, share it with the parent or caregiver so they can decide whether to pass it on, and if a family asks you to speak with a counselor, describe specific observations, including what happened, when it happened, and what came before it.

Connecting with community resources

It is helpful to know local resources before they are needed. Most areas have a community mental health center, family support and advocacy organizations, and youth programs. In most of the United States, dialing 211 connects callers with local health and social services.

Recommended

  • Ask parents which strategies work at home
  • Share specific, factual observations
  • Keep family information confidential
  • Point out strengths you have noticed

Avoid

  • Attempting to diagnose a child
  • Contacting a school or provider without the family’s knowledge
  • Discussing a child’s challenges with other parents
  • Offering unrequested advice

Section 4

Transition to adulthood

What changes after high school

While a student is in school, services are organized for them. For example, an IEP team meets each year, school staff monitor progress, and parents make most decisions. However, things change after graduation: adult mental health providers, colleges, and employers generally expect young people to identify their own needs and request support. At age 18, young people become legal adults, and parents typically cannot access their health information or make medical decisions for them without their consent.

Research on post-school outcomes shows why this period requires attention. Youth with emotional disturbances trail the general population in positive transition outcomes, and their high rates of involvement with the justice system point to the need for earlier support (Wagner & Newman, 2012).

Factors linked to positive outcomes

Researchers have identified 23 in-school predictors of improved outcomes in employment, education, and independent living for students with disabilities (Mazzotti et al., 2021). Several involve experiences that take place in the community, as shown below in the graphic.

PredictorHow community members can help
Paid work experienceLocal employers can offer part-time jobs, job shadowing, or internships.

How community members can support transition

Maintain contact through the transition

Many supports end at graduation, so having a coach, mentor, or faith leader who stays in touch after high school provides continuity that formal systems often cannot.

Provide work and volunteer opportunities

Employers and organizations can offer first jobs, volunteer roles, or internships with clear expectations and specific feedback, including behavior-specific praise, that help young workers succeed.

Encourage self-advocacy

Young adults with EBD need to be able to describe what helps them to professors, employers, and health care providers. Adults can build this skill by asking young people what works for them and respecting their answers.

Questions young adults may want to consider: Who will provide my care after I leave my pediatrician or child therapist? How do I refill my own prescriptions? Who should have access to my health information? Where can I go if I need help again?

Reflection

Reflection

I chose the general public as the audience for this toolkit because of a gap I found early in my research. Forness et al. (2012) estimate that about 12 percent of students need support for emotional or behavioral concerns at any given time, yet fewer than 1 percent are served in special education under the category of emotional disturbance. Most young people with EBD are never formally identified. They still spend much of their time in community settings such as sports teams, youth groups, jobs, and extended family gatherings, where the adults around them usually have no training in EBD. Teachers, counselors, and clinicians already have professional resources. I wanted to create something for the coaches, relatives, mentors, and employers who see these young people regularly but have little guidance.

This audience affected the content of each section. In the overview, I kept the federal definition but focused more on recognizing behavior patterns and understanding behavior as communication. I included the distinction between externalizing and internalizing behaviors because members of the public are more likely to notice disruptive behavior and miss signs of anxiety or depression. I also included common misconceptions, since beliefs such as “it’s just bad parenting” can discourage families from seeking help.

For the strategies section, I selected practices that adults can use without professional training or special materials. Many evidence-based interventions for EBD, such as functional behavior assessments, require a trained team and a structured setting. Mental Health First Aid was developed specifically for community members, and Morgan et al. (2018) found that it improves knowledge, confidence, and helping behavior while reducing stigma. Behavior-specific praise and offering choices were studied mainly in classrooms (Lane et al., 2018; Royer et al., 2019), but both can be used on a sports field, at home, or at work. I included concrete examples for each strategy so readers could apply them right away.

The collaboration section required the most thought because community members are not usually part of formal treatment teams. The wraparound model was useful here because it intentionally includes natural supports such as relatives, coaches, and faith leaders (Suter & Bruns, 2009). This showed me that the general public already has a recognized role in coordinated care. I also wanted to address the limits of that role. Community members should respect family privacy and allow information to pass through the family rather than contacting schools or providers directly.

The transition section focused on what happens when school-based services end. Wagner and Newman (2012) found that youth with emotional disturbances have poorer post-school outcomes than their peers, including higher rates of justice system involvement. Mazzotti et al. (2021) identified predictors of post-school success, and several of them, including paid work experience and community involvement, take place outside of school. Local employers and mentors can contribute to transition outcomes even though they are not part of formal transition planning.

I chose a website format because it fits how the general public is likely to use this information. A website is easier to share than a printed handbook and can be read on a phone when a specific situation comes up. The visuals, including the iceberg diagram, the prevalence graphic, and the support team diagram, present key ideas for readers who may not be familiar with research terminology.

Overall, I believe this toolkit can help community members respond to young people with EBD in a more informed and consistent way. It gives them a basic understanding of EBD, practical strategies supported by research, and guidance on how to support families without overstepping. Because so many young people with EBD are never formally identified, the adults they encounter in everyday settings play an important role in their support.
